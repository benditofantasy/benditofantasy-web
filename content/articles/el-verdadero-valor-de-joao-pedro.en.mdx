Friends, we're back to talk about how things are going in Fantasy, following a gameweek that many of us would probably rather forget. One of those weeks when you look at your team, check the points you've managed to collect, and start wondering whether there was anything you could realistically have done differently. And while we could spend this entire article regretting our decisions, I think there's a much more interesting story behind one of the biggest disappointments of the gameweek: Chelsea's defeat to Tottenham.

On paper, this looked like exactly the kind of fixture that encouraged us to trust Chelsea's attacking players. Tottenham were visiting Stamford Bridge, a stadium where they've historically struggled to win, while Chelsea had several players whose individual quality and attacking involvement seemed to offer plenty of reasons to back them. Some of us even considered Chelsea assets for the captaincy ahead of Erling Haaland, who faced a difficult trip to Aston Villa. But the match told a completely different story. Tottenham won **2–0**, Chelsea barely threatened, and several of the players we'd placed our faith in delivered very little.

What's interesting is that this defeat didn't happen in isolation. Chelsea had also lost **3–0 to Brentford**, and both matches share an absence that's beginning to attract attention: **João Pedro**. Of course, we're talking about just two games. It would be premature to conclude that Chelsea can't compete without him or that his absence alone explains those defeats. There are far too many variables in football to reduce a result to one player. Still, when we look at how the team played in those matches, a difference begins to emerge that goes well beyond whoever happens to occupy the centre-forward position. And perhaps that's where the real story lies.

## The Striker Who Makes Everyone Else Better

When we talk about João Pedro, our first instinct as Fantasy managers is naturally to look at his goals, assists, minutes, and opportunities inside the box. After all, we're evaluating a forward, and we want to know how many points he can produce. But some players offer value to their teams that isn't fully reflected in their individual statistics, and João Pedro appears to be one of them. Chelsea don't just need their striker to finish moves; they need him to help build them, connect the midfield with the attack, and allow the team to progress even when the opposition manages to put them under pressure.

João Pedro has a particularly interesting ability to leave the traditional number-nine position, drop toward midfield, and make himself available when Chelsea are trying to play through an opposition press. He doesn't simply receive the ball with his back to goal; he knows how to protect it, find the best-positioned teammate, and keep possession alive when another player might lose it. When an opponent presses Chelsea, there isn't always an easy forward pass available. Midfielders can find themselves surrounded, the wide areas can be closed off, and defenders need an outlet that allows them to move the ball forward without giving it away. That's where João Pedro comes in: he drops a few yards, receives in uncomfortable spaces, withstands physical pressure, and connects one line of the team with the next.

Not all of those actions lead to an assist. Some don't even make it into the match highlights, but they allow Chelsea to retain possession in the opposition half, give teammates time to join the attack, and start the next phase of play several yards closer to goal. At times, it's a role reminiscent of what Roberto Firmino used to do for Jürgen Klopp's Liverpool. Not because they're identical players, but because both can offer something that's difficult to find: **a striker who improves the team's collective play by leaving his position, rather than simply moving further away from goal**.

There's another important consequence to those movements. When João Pedro drops deep, he forces opposing centre-backs to make a decision. If one follows him, space opens up behind the defensive line; if neither does, the striker can receive freely and turn. And when he drifts wide, he can drag defenders out of position and reshape the opposition's defensive structure. Those small movements are what eventually create opportunities for players like Cole Palmer, Morgan Rogers, and Pedro Neto.

> João Pedro's true value isn't just in the moves he finishes, but also in all the ones he allows his teammates to start.

## The Domino Effect on Palmer and Rogers

This is where João Pedro's absence starts to become particularly interesting from a Fantasy perspective. When a player is unavailable, it isn't necessarily just his individual output that disappears; the conditions in which his teammates produce can change as well. Think about Cole Palmer. He's at his most dangerous when he receives the ball in areas where he can decide a move: finding the final pass, combining around the penalty area, or going directly for goal. We want him involved in those final yards, not constantly trying to work out how to move the ball from midfield into attack.

However, when Chelsea lose the striker who normally connects those lines, someone has to take on part of that responsibility, and Palmer is one of the players talented enough to do it. The problem is that **being capable of building an attack doesn't necessarily mean that's the role we want him playing**. If Palmer has to drop deeper to receive, help beat the press, and organise possession, he's certainly involved in the game, but he may also be moving away from the situations that normally make him such an attractive Fantasy asset.

Something similar happens with Morgan Rogers, although from a different perspective. Rogers can be extremely dangerous when he finds space to carry the ball, take on defenders, or arrive from a deeper position. His power and ability to progress with the ball are qualities Chelsea can exploit once they've beaten the initial press, but asking him to replace João Pedro's functions means changing his role. Against Tottenham, Xabi Alonso chose to use him as the striker, with Geovany Quenda occupying one of the wide positions. On paper, the idea had a certain logic: movement, positional rotations, and players capable of attacking different spaces. In practice, Chelsea lost much of the connection that normally allows them to establish themselves in the opposition half.

This isn't about saying Rogers can't play as a false nine. It's about recognising that **occupying the same position doesn't mean performing the same function**. João Pedro can receive with his back to goal, hold his own in physical duels, secure a difficult ball, and give the rest of the team time to advance. Rogers offers different qualities, and when those characteristics change, so does what Chelsea can do in possession. Suddenly, Palmer has to operate further from goal, Rogers is no longer attacking certain spaces from his usual position, and the runs of players like Neto can go unrewarded because the pass that would normally find them never arrives. It's a domino effect in which the other players don't necessarily become worse footballers, but they do begin operating under less favourable conditions.

> Occupying the same position doesn't mean performing the same function.

## Tottenham Faced a Different Chelsea

The match against Tottenham provides a fairly clear example of what we're discussing, although it's worth repeating that one performance doesn't, by itself, prove a structural dependency. Roberto De Zerbi's team managed to dictate the terms of the contest. Chelsea struggled to keep the ball, progress into the opposition half, and establish sustained attacks. It wasn't simply a finishing problem: much of the time, they couldn't even create the situations in which we'd normally expect their attacking players to become dangerous.

The numbers help put that into perspective. Chelsea finished with just **7 shots and 0.43 expected goals (xG)**, compared with **15 shots** for Tottenham. Beyond the scoreline, that's an extremely limited attacking output for a team playing at home with several important attacking players available. And there's a fundamental difference between having the ball and knowing what to do with it. A team can lose possession because it attempts risky passes, because its attackers fail to control the ball, or because the opposition executes an outstanding press. But it can also lose possession because it lacks the player who normally provides an outlet, offers a moment of relief under pressure, and turns a difficult ball into an opportunity to move forward.

It's the kind of work that often goes unnoticed when the striker is available. We get used to seeing it, take it for granted, and end up paying attention only to who took the shot or provided the final pass. Then that player disappears, and we discover that moves that once looked straightforward are no longer quite so simple. Against Tottenham, Chelsea struggled precisely with that transition between winning or starting possession and getting the ball to their attackers in favourable situations. They couldn't establish themselves consistently around the opposition penalty area and spent too much of the match trying to regain control. Palmer was eventually sent off for two yellow cards, in a night that also reflected the frustration of a team unable to find answers.

It would be unfair to blame Rogers for failing to reproduce João Pedro's characteristics, just as it would be excessive to attribute the entire defeat to one absence. Tottenham deserve credit for their own performance, their preparation, and the way they exploited Chelsea's difficulties. But the question remains valid: how much of what we saw came down to the quality of the opposition, and how much had to do with the absence of a player who normally makes Chelsea's attacking play function more smoothly? Two matches can't give us a definitive answer, but they do give us a reason to keep watching.

## What This Changes for Fantasy

This is the part that matters most to us as managers. During the week, many of us looked at Chelsea from a fairly logical perspective. Tottenham were an opponent who had historically struggled to win at Stamford Bridge, Chelsea's attacking players looked appealing, and there were reasons to consider one of them for the captaincy. I saw that opportunity myself and **gave Morgan Rogers the armband ahead of Haaland**, who had a demanding fixture against Aston Villa. But a decision that seemed reasonable with João Pedro available could look very different once his absence was confirmed.

And that's something we need to incorporate into our analysis. We're used to evaluating players through their minutes, individual numbers, and fixture difficulty. All of that matters, but perhaps we don't always pay enough attention to the relationships between players on the same team. A footballer can remain a guaranteed starter, keep the same price, and still be his club's most talented attacking player, yet find himself in a completely different situation when the teammate who normally helps him receive the ball near goal is missing.

**João Pedro's availability could become an important factor when evaluating Palmer and Rogers**, especially if Chelsea continue to show similar difficulties in upcoming matches. That doesn't mean we should automatically sell Palmer every time João Pedro is unavailable, or that Rogers suddenly stops being an interesting option. That would be an overreaction to a sample that's still far too small. What it does mean is that we need to distinguish between a player's individual quality and the collective conditions that allow him to produce.

If João Pedro is available, Chelsea may recover the reference point that helps them progress the ball, draw defenders out of position, and create space for teammates. If he isn't, we'll have to watch whether Xabi Alonso finds a different solution, adjusts the team's structure, or identifies someone who can take on those responsibilities without sacrificing too much attacking presence. Because the problem isn't simply finding someone to play up front; it's finding someone who makes the attack work. And for Fantasy, that distinction may ultimately matter far more than the name listed in the starting eleven.

That's why, after what we saw against Brentford and Tottenham, there's one question I think is particularly relevant for the coming gameweeks: **if João Pedro is unavailable again, would we still trust Palmer and Rogers in quite the same way?**

> We need to distinguish between a player's individual quality and the collective conditions that allow him to produce.

## Final Whistle

While Chelsea search for answers on the pitch, at Manchester United the questions increasingly seem to be directed toward the dugout. Michael Carrick's future is once again under discussion, in a situation where results, selection decisions, and signs of tension around the team have fuelled the debate about the direction of the project. The situation involving Bruno Fernandes, one of the most influential figures in the dressing room, isn't helping keep that conversation focused exclusively on football either.

But there's something about all of this that catches my attention. Every time Manchester United go through a crisis, the most immediate solution seems to be finding the next manager. Names emerge, new ideas are discussed, and the potential change is presented as the beginning of yet another rebuild. And perhaps it could be. Not every manager is right for every project, and there comes a point when results have to matter as well. Still, after so many attempts, it's worth asking whether Manchester United really need another manager or whether they need to solve problems that will remain regardless of who's sitting in the dugout. Because replacing the person in charge may be necessary; the difficult part is making sure it doesn't become, once again, just another temporary fix.
