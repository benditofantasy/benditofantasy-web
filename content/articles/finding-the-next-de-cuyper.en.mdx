A look at William Osula, two overlooked midfielders, and what to check before buying a player the crowd has barely noticed.

By the time an FPL player appears in every group chat, has survived three YouTube thumbnails and is being described as ‘essential’, the secret may be out. Which is fine if you already own him. Slightly less fine if you’ve just watched his price climb while your transfer budget stays exactly where it was.

Brighton’s Maxim De Cuyper is a useful example. He has impressed in a different role, picked up points and attracted attention. His FPL price has climbed from £4.5m to £5.5m, and around 48% of managers now own him. The question isn’t whether he was a good discovery. **It’s how to recognise a similar opportunity before the rest of FPL notices.**

That is the angle I wanted to explore with Fantasy Matchup Edge (FME): not a list of guaranteed hidden gems, but players whose underlying opportunities look more interesting than their popularity suggests — and a few tests worth applying before taking the plunge.

## The player I keep coming back to: William Osula

Newcastle’s William Osula costs £5.8m and is owned by just 0.4% of FPL managers. He has started four of the first ten league games, so this isn’t a straightforward ‘buy him immediately’ recommendation. Far from it.

Yet he sits sixth across the entire Premier League in FME’s Next 5 projections: **27.72 expected points over GW11–GW15, or 5.54 per match**, conditional on him starting each one. That is a surprising position for a striker who has barely troubled the ownership charts.

The first obstacle is obvious. Newcastle host Arsenal in GW11, and FME gives Osula 4.80 expected points and a 12.0% chance of reaching ten or more. After that come Brighton away, Manchester United at home, Sunderland at home and Ipswich away. It isn’t a promise of four goals or four easy fixtures. It is a reason to keep him on the watchlist beyond one tricky weekend.

He has already shown what he can do: he scored nine FPL points in Newcastle’s 2–0 win at Crystal Palace in GW8. But that one game is not why the model likes him. FME considers a player’s wider action profile and role alongside team supply, opponent tendencies and relevant match environments. Recent performances matter, but one afternoon doesn’t suddenly rewrite an entire footballer.

**The biggest uncertainty is playing time.** If Osula is starting consistently by GW12 or GW13, this could look like a very different discussion. If he keeps sharing minutes, the appealing five-match total will remain just that: a conditional projection.

## Two more who are hiding in plain sight

The current gameweek gives us two very different kinds of overlooked midfielders.

Christos Tzolis is projected for 6.24 FPL points against Newcastle, but sits in only 3.9% of squads at £6.1m. Noah Okafor, meanwhile, is just 0.3% owned at £5.7m despite a 5.77-point forecast away at Chelsea. Those are interesting figures precisely because neither fixture screams ‘easy’ from a traditional red-and-green ticker.

> A good individual matchup is not always the same as an easy match for the team.

Different opponents allow different actions in different areas. If the player’s attacking profile fits those openings, there can be a route to points even when the fixture looks uncomfortable on paper.

Neither should be declared the next must-have on those numbers alone. **A projection can identify opportunity without guaranteeing a goal, an assist or 90 minutes.** And if they have not yet delivered the explosive return everyone wants, that doesn’t necessarily invalidate the underlying case. It means we need to keep checking whether the opportunities are still there.

There are others worth monitoring too. Bournemouth’s Eli Junior Kroupi is projected for 5.87 against Forest at just 0.7% ownership; Bournemouth’s Rayan is on 5.21 with 2.4% ownership. These are watchlist candidates, not a demand to fill every midfield slot with differentials.

## Five signs I would look for before the bandwagon arrives

The temptation is to spot a low ownership figure and a big projection and immediately shout ‘differential’. That can work. It can also turn your team into an expensive collection of hopeful substitutes.

**First, look for repeatable actions:** shots, chances created, useful defensive work or involvement in dangerous areas. One goal from one shot is fun, but it isn’t much of a foundation by itself.

**Second, ask whether the matchup suits the player rather than just the club.** **Third, pay close attention to starts, substitutions, injuries and role changes.** Osula’s ranking makes him intriguing; his minutes are what make him risky.

**Fourth, look beyond the next 90 minutes.** A five-game projection is more useful for planning a transfer than reacting to a single brilliant Saturday, although every future score still assumes the player starts. **Fifth, use ownership and price movement as market context, not proof of quality.** De Cuyper’s current popularity is evidence that managers noticed him; it does not explain why his performances improved.

FME’s FPL player projections, Next 5 fixture difficulty and price-and-ownership trends let you check those different parts of the story separately. **The idea is to get a little ahead of the crowd — without getting ahead of the evidence.**

## Being early and being right are not the same thing

> Being early and being right are not the same thing.

The next De Cuyper might be Osula. It might be Tzolis, Okafor or somebody not on this list. More likely, the useful lesson is the process: find a player whose underlying involvement and upcoming matchups look promising, then wait for enough evidence that his role and minutes support the gamble.

Sometimes that means acting before the goals arrive. Sometimes it means missing the first price rise while you check the player is actually going to play. I can live with that. Getting in early is lovely; getting in early for the wrong reasons is just another way of being wrong ahead of schedule.
